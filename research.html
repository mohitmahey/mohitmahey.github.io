Research

Finding the site of action of indaziflam

Indaziflam is used widely, and yet where exactly it acts inside a plant has stayed unknown. It is classified as a cellulose-biosynthesis inhibitor, yet it behaves differently from other known CBIs.

My PhD research was to answer that question from several directions at once. I combined cytology, biochemistry and transcriptomics on treated and untreated Poa annua, and ran molecular docking as an orthogonal in-silico check on the candidate targets. I also looked at whether indaziflam does anything outside the plant kingdom.

Cytochrome P450s and multiple herbicide resistance

Poa annua populations survive several herbicide chemistries at once, including ones they have never been exposed to. That pattern points away from a mutated target site and towards metabolic resistance: the plant is degrading the herbicide before it can act or reducing the effective concentration.

Gene co-expression network analysis across resistant and susceptible populations narrowed the field to a set of candidate cytochrome P450s. A developmental expression atlas for grape powdery mildew

Erysiphe necator causes powdery mildew on grapevine, and like the weeds I worked on before it, it has been steadily evolving resistance to survive the chemistry such as quinoxyfen.

My first postdoctoral work builds a developmental expression atlas across resistant and susceptible isolates, using spatial transcriptomics. Bulk RNA-seq averages over exactly the spatial detail that matters when a pathogen's life stages are morphologically distinct and physically separated.

The atlas will be released publicly.

Oak wilt: genome and population structure

Bretziella fagacearum is the fungus behind oak wilt, a disease serious enough in the American Midwest. Managing it well means knowing how the pathogen is moving and that requires a good reference genome.

I contributed to the high-quality genome resource for B. fagacearum published in PhytoFrontiers, and I am now using it to characterise population diversity and structure across the United States. The question is whether oak wilt is spreading as one connected population or as distinct regional introductions, because those two scenarios call for quite different management responses.

Population genomics of invasive and resistant plants

Resistance is a population-level phenomenon before it is a molecular one, so a good deal of my work has been reconstructing where problem populations came from and how they are connected.

I have looked at Amaranthus palmeri's invasion of the Mediterranean, tracing the structure of introduced populations against their North American source. This was done to understnd the invasion of the world's most destructive weed across Europe.

Erythroxylum coca across Colombia, to understand spread of this traditonally valubel plant and how its spread across the country. and, with colleagues, at glyphosate resistance in horseweed (Erigeron canadensis) where drought stress and herbicide response intersect. Tha aim was to understand how increasing risk of drought can pose increased risk of reduction in herbicide efficacy.

Rethinking HRAC group 15

As a visiting scholar with Dr. Rebecka Dücker at Georg-August University of Göttingen, I worked on the reclassification of HRAC group 15 herbicides.

The experimental side involved expressing KCS genes in yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) and running herbicide feeding assays against them, then extracting fatty acids and profiling them by GC-MS.
